Phi & psi angles

The peptide backbone can only bend in two places per residue. Turn those two angles by hand, watch the atoms run into each other, and the Ramachandran plot draws itself.

Drag the φ and ψ sliders, or drag straight on the plot — the model and the marker stay locked together. When two atoms come within van der Waals contact the pair is flagged in the 3D view with the distance in ångströms, so a forbidden region on the plot always has a specific collision behind it. Every preset is a real conformation: try α-helix, then Eclipsed, and compare.

The shaded background is not a traced figure. It is computed when the page loads by testing this exact molecule at 16,200 combinations of φ and ψ — so the plot and the clashes can never disagree with each other.

    Two angles describe a residue

    The peptide bond is rigid: the six atoms Cα–C′(=O)–N(–H)–Cα lie in a plane because the C′–N bond has partial double-bond character. So a polypeptide is a chain of stiff plates, and the only places it can bend are the two single bonds at each Cα. Those two rotations are φ and ψ. Give every residue its φ and ψ and you have specified the entire backbone fold.

    Reading the sign

    A torsion is measured by looking straight down the rotating bond. Rotating the far end clockwise is positive. Both angles are 0° when the two outer bonds eclipse each other — press the Eclipsed preset and you will see the worst collision on the whole map, which is exactly why the centre of the plot is empty.

    Where the forbidden zones come from

    Nothing about the plot is arbitrary. Atoms are spheres, and spheres cannot overlap. Sweep φ toward 0° and the carbonyl oxygen of the preceding residue drives into the amide hydrogen of the next one. Sweep ψ toward 0° and the same oxygen meets its own residue's amide. Roughly nine tenths of the plot is ruled out this way — before chemistry, before hydrogen bonding, purely because the atoms will not fit.

    Why the left half is the busy half

    Cβ is the culprit. Alanine's side chain sits on one specific side of Cα because the residue is the L isomer, and it blocks most of the φ > 0 half of the map. Turn on the van der Waals shells and drag into the right-hand side to watch it happen. Glycine, which has no Cβ, is the one residue that roams freely there — which is why glycine turns up wherever a chain needs to double back.

    The three islands

    The lower-left basin near (−57, −47) is the right-handed α-helix. The broad upper-left region around (−139, +135) is β-sheet, and the fully extended chain sits out at the corners. The small strained island at (+57, +47) is the left-handed α-helix — reachable, but tight enough that few residues other than glycine bother.

    What this model leaves out

    Bond lengths and angles are held at ideal values, ω is pinned at 180° (trans), and the side-chain methyl is kept staggered. The atoms are hard spheres, so there is no electrostatics and no hydrogen bonding — a real energy map softens these edges and adds a shallow bridge between the β and αR basins. Real proteins also sit slightly inside the strained zones, because a little strain here is worth paying for a good hydrogen bond somewhere else.

    Geometry is generated from ideal internal coordinates and rotated with an exact torsion construction, so the angles you read are the angles in the model. The plot is a hard-sphere map computed on a 2° grid from the very same non-bonded atom pairs the 3D view checks; the contact criterion follows Ramachandran, Ramakrishnan & Sasisekharan (1963) in spirit, using a fraction of the summed van der Waals radii that you can adjust.
